How Raymore Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Most premature tank failures start with maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Drain and flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and test the relief valve each year so it doesn't seize. Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year to protect the heat exchanger.

How old is your water heater? The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age says a lot about what comes next. Most tank models make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units about 20. If your heater is beyond that point and is also showing discolored water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Take a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank steel, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first sign of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a flooded utility room.

Common Water Heater Problems in Raymore

Most water heater problems we see in Raymore come back to the water itself. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Age matters just as much. Plenty of homes in the area still have the original tank, and once a tank is past the ten-year mark, the anode rod is usually spent and rust starts working on the steel itself. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Homes on private wells can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank on closed systems, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and seismic straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less to address early.

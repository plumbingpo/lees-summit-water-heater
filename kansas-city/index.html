Keeping Your Kansas City Water Heater Running Longer

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which reduces the risk of scalding and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the month and year it was built, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank heaters reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units around 20. If your heater is beyond that point and also shows rusty water, popping sounds, or moisture at the base, it's usually due for a planned replacement, and that's almost always cheaper and easier on everyone than an emergency replacement once the tank fails.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to high water pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip ignored for months can wear out the valve and stress the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Hot Water Headaches Kansas City Homeowners Face

Around Kansas City, water heater trouble usually trace back to a handful of causes. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the protective anode rod is gone, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the steel begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Well water can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older homes often need code upgrades when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak could damage finished space, and seismic straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
